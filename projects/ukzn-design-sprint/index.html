The design problem

The challenge

Digital learning development needed shared design standards and practical faculty support, informed by root-cause analysis with academic leadership. College surveys, problem-tree and objective-tree workshops, benchmarking against four universities and a digital literacy survey with 1,633 respondents set the priorities: mobile-first, bilingual and differentiated learning.

The logic

Diagnose before designing, then teach design by doing. Every session and sprint ends in a working deliverable, and shared standards and toolkits keep the work consistent after the workshop ends.

What lecturers produce

Redesigned modules: learning outcomes frameworks, storyboards and lesson plans, assessment plans and rubrics, accessible layouts and improvement plans.

My role

I led a team of four instructional designers, established design standards, supported continuous professional development and worked with university leadership on digital learning strategy. I designed the Module Design Sprints programme and the E-Learning for Lecturers course.

From design to delivery

Workshops and sprints

A five-part faculty development workshop series drew on ADDIE, SAM and multimedia learning principles, giving faculty practical cycles for developing modules. The Module Design Sprints ran as a 10-week online programme of five two-hour sprints.

E-Learning for Lecturers

A five-week, self-paced Moodle course with in-video questions, peer review of each other’s activities and an evaluation prompt after every unit.

Standards and quality

Blended Teaching, Student Engagement and EdTech toolkits supported academic staff, and a Blended Module Style Guide set accessibility rules for alt text, captions and transcripts, and isiZulu translation of instructions where possible. I worked with Quality Assurance on NQF-compliant curriculum and digital learning practices.

The Module Design Sprints

Five sprints, five deliverables.

Ten weeks online, five two-hour sprints, each ending in a working piece of the lecturer’s own module.

01

Outcomes

A learning outcomes framework for the module.

02

Storyboard

A module storyboard and lesson plan built on those outcomes.

03

Assessment

An assessment plan and rubric aligned to the outcomes.

04

Layout

An accessible module layout that follows the shared style guide.

05

Improvement

An improvement action plan for the next run of the module.

Design decisions

Every element has a job.

The programme was designed so that faculty development produced real modules, not just attendance.

DecisionWhat it trainsIn the programme
Diagnose before designing Setting priorities from evidence rather than assumption. College surveys, problem- and objective-tree workshops, benchmarking against four universities and a 1,633-respondent digital literacy survey.
Design for the students actually there Mobile-first, bilingual and differentiated learning. The priorities that came out of the diagnosis shaped every programme.
Every sprint ends in a deliverable Lecturers leaving with working parts of their own module. Outcomes, storyboard, assessment, layout and improvement plan.
Units that open in sequence Pacing, with feedback before moving on. In E-Learning for Lecturers, each unit opens only once the previous unit’s evaluation is complete.
Peer review Learning from colleagues’ designs. Lecturers review each other’s activities during the course.
Standards, not one-off training Consistency after the workshop ends. A Blended Module Style Guide with accessibility rules and isiZulu translation of instructions where possible.
Evidence

What’s documented

14 workshops with 297 attendees, 21 module redesigns across four colleges, and the university’s inaugural Instructional Design Expo.

Limits

What the evidence can’t show

These are measures of participation and output. They don’t show how teaching practice or student outcomes changed in the redesigned modules.
