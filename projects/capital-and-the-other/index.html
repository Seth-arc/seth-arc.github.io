Capital & The Other

Playtested · grant-funded

An educational board game for an undergraduate economics course. Players build a life through four forms of capital, while their identity quietly shapes what each of them can reach.

← Explore all projects
Learners
Undergraduates in William & Mary’s Economics of Identity course: race, class, caste and gender
Funding
William & Mary Learn, Discover, Innovate Grant, 2024–2025
Format
Physical board game: player profiles, cards, tokens, an in-game reflection phase and a facilitated debrief
Shared at
William & Mary Teaching & Learning Symposium, 2025
The design problem

The challenge

Identity economics is easy to state and hard to feel. Undergraduates can repeat that race, class, caste and gender shape access to resources without ever experiencing how that plays out over time.

The logic

Map the module’s threshold concepts to game mechanics before designing any components. Then give every player a profile with a different identity and different starting capital, so structural differences show up in play rather than in a lecture.

What players produce

Decisions on the board, written reflections during play, and a facilitated debrief that connects what happened at the table to the course concepts.

My role

I designed the game and its evaluation, ran the playtest and wrote the project report that set the next iteration.

Four forms of capital

Financial

Money and assets: what most people mean by capital, and the easiest to see.

Social

Networks and relationships that open doors.

Cultural

Knowledge of norms, language and cultural fluency in the settings that matter.

Human

Education and skills, and the time and money it takes to build them.

Evaluation into redesign

What the playtest changed.

Pre-, mid- and post-game surveys and observation notes pointed to specific changes for the next version.

01

Interaction carried the game

All five playtesters named interacting with other players as the most engaging part of play.

02

Reflection broke the flow

Four of five rated recording reflection prompts the least engaging part, though it was the most deliberate learning mechanism. Next version: move reflection into the debrief.

03

Winning was ambiguous

Was success the most capital, or the deepest understanding? The tension sparked discussion but needs an explicit answer at larger scale.

04

Make structure felt

Players wanted identity to restrict access, not just starting values. Next version: identity-based limits on certain board spaces and cards.

05

Weight the capitals

Next version: financial capital takes more tokens for the same impact, closer to how economic hierarchies actually work.

06

Sharpen the profiles

Next version: profiles with explicit objectives, and tighter links between a player’s background and how they gain capital.

Design decisions

Every mechanic has a job.

The game makes players live through the course concepts rather than recall them.

DecisionWhat it trainsIn the game
Identity as a structural variable Seeing how identity shapes access to opportunity. Each profile fixes identity attributes and starting capital before the first turn.
Four capitals, not one Distinguishing financial, social, cultural and human capital. Players track all four on their profile sheet as the game goes on.
Concepts first, components second Keeping every mechanic tied to a learning outcome. The module’s threshold concepts were mapped to mechanics before any physical components were designed.
One shared table Learning through negotiation with other players. Players interact on one board with shared cards and tokens. In the playtest this was the most engaging element.
A reflection phase Noticing what the game is showing, not just playing it. Players record reflection prompts during play. The playtest suggests moving this into the debrief.
Physical, not digital Embodied, social play. Board, cards, tokens and profile sheets. All five playtesters preferred the physical format.
Evidence

What was measured

Five students playtested the game with pre-, mid- and post-game surveys, plus observation notes. Across the core concepts, the share of self-ratings at “strong understanding” rose from 23% before the game to 50% after. All five rated the experience excellent.

Limits

What the evidence can’t show

Five participants, all of whom had already completed the course, so the playtest was a return to familiar material rather than a first encounter. That may have lifted engagement. The gains are self-reported; content assessments would be needed to show measurable learning.
